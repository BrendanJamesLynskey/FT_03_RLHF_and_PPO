01

From SFT to RLHF — What Supervised Can't Fix

SFT teaches the model to imitate its training data. If the training data contains helpful responses, the model learns to produce helpful-looking responses. But imitation has a structural ceiling: you can only be as good as your data labels, and human labellers are expensive, inconsistent, and unable to evaluate subtle quality differences between nearly-equal responses.

What SFT leaves on the table

  • Ranking among good responses: labellers write one gold answer but cannot easily rank 16 generated candidates
  • Subtle helpfulness signals: "concise but complete" vs "verbose but exhaustive" is hard to encode as a single label
  • Refusal calibration: SFT models either over-refuse or under-refuse; the sweet spot requires a preference signal
  • Sycophancy: SFT data written to please annotators bakes in agreement with the human regardless of correctness

What RLHF adds

  • Preference learning: humans rank outputs; the reward model learns a scalar from comparative judgements, not absolute scores
  • Optimisation against the reward: PPO generates many candidate completions, scores them, and updates toward high-reward behaviour
  • KL anchor: the KL penalty keeps the policy close to the SFT reference — prevents the model from exploiting reward model blind spots
  • Online exploration: the model generates novel outputs and gets feedback; SFT is purely offline
Historical context

RLHF was described by OpenAI in the InstructGPT paper (2022), then refined in their early work on summarisation from human feedback (Stiennon et al., 2020). Anthropic extended it with Constitutional AI (2022). The core PPO loop has been largely stable since then — later work (DPO, GRPO) mostly replaces or simplifies it.

Reward Modelling — Pairwise Preferences, Bradley-Terry

A reward model (RM) is a language model with its final next-token-prediction head replaced by a single scalar output layer. It takes a (prompt, completion) pair and outputs a scalar score indicating quality. Training data is pairwise comparisons: for each prompt, human raters see two completions and pick the preferred one.

Bradley-Terry Loss

Given a chosen completion yw ("winner") and a rejected completion yl ("loser") for prompt x, the Bradley-Terry model defines the probability that yw is preferred:

RM base model sizeSame as policy or 2–3× smallerLarger RM better calibrates fine-grained quality; smaller RM trains faster, fits on fewer GPUs
Preference collectionHuman raters, LLM-as-judge (RLAIF)Human: higher quality, expensive; LLM: cheap, scalable, but inherits model biases
Ties / equal pairsDiscard or model as 0.5 probabilityDiscarding is simpler; modelling ties reduces dataset waste by ~15 %

The PPO Loop — Actor, Critic, Reward, KL Penalty

Proximal Policy Optimisation (Schulman et al., 2017) is the RL algorithm at the heart of most RLHF implementations. In the LLM context it operates on entire completion sequences as single actions.

1. Rollout: Actor (πθ) generates completion y given prompt x
↓
2. Reward: r(x,y) from reward model; KL penalty: −β log(πθ(y|x) / πref(y|x))
↓
3. Advantage estimate: At via GAE using Critic Vφ(x)
↓
4. PPO update: clip((πθ/πold)·A, 1−ε, 1+ε) — prevents large policy jumps
↓
5. Critic update: minimise (Vφ(x) − returns)2
↑
Repeat for N epochs per rollout batch; then collect fresh rollouts
The KL penalty is not optional

The total reward is rtotal = rRM(x,y) − β · KL(πθ || πref). Without the KL penalty the actor quickly discovers degenerate completions that fool the reward model — this is reward hacking. The reference policy πref is the SFT model, frozen throughout PPO. A typical starting value for β is 0.02–0.1; higher values keep the policy closer to SFT at the cost of reward-signal responsiveness.

04

Hyperparameters — KL Coefficient, Clip Ratio, GAE Lambda

PPO has more hyperparameters than SFT, and they interact. Here are the ones that matter most and sensible starting ranges for LLM RLHF.

HyperparameterSymbolTypical rangeEffect of raising
KL penalty coefficientβ0.02–0.2More conservative; policy stays closer to SFT; slower reward improvement
PPO clip ratioε0.1–0.2Allows larger policy steps; can destabilise training if >0.3
GAE lambdaλ0.9–0.99Higher: lower variance, higher bias in advantage estimates; 0.95 is a good default
Rollout batch size—64–512Larger = more stable gradient estimates; must fit in VRAM during rollout phase
PPO epochs per rolloutK1–4More reuse of rollout data; risk of off-policy distribution shift beyond K=4
Value loss coefficientcv0.1–1.0Weights critic update relative to actor; 0.1 is typical for LLMs (critic is secondary)
Entropy bonusce0.0–0.01Encourages exploration; prevents premature mode collapse on the output distribution
Adaptive KL (OpenAI approach)

Rather than fixing β, set a target KL divergence (e.g. 0.05 nats/token) and adjust β up if KL exceeds target, down if KL is too low. This is implemented in TRL's PPOTrainer via target_kl and adaptive_kl_ctrl=True. The adaptive controller prevents both runaway divergence (high KL, reward hacking) and stagnation (near-zero KL, no learning).

05

Failure Modes — Reward Hacking, Mode Collapse, KL Spike

PPO for LLMs has a richer failure-mode taxonomy than standard RL because the action space (natural language) is so vast and the reward signal (a neural network) is so gameable.

Reward Hacking

The policy finds completions that score highly on the reward model but are clearly bad to humans. Classic examples: repetitive praise (the RM was trained on helpful-sounding text → model learns to produce effusive compliments), length bias (if RM conflates length with quality, model outputs extremely long responses), format exploitation (bullet points score higher → model bullet-points everything).

Mitigations: KL penalty, stronger RM, diverse preference data, reward normalisation.

Mode Collapse

The policy collapses to a narrow set of safe, generic completions. The model learns "I can't help with that" or "That's a great question!" are safe high-reward responses and over-produces them.

Mitigations: entropy bonus in PPO objective, diversity reward, broad prompt distribution in rollouts, curriculum (easier prompts first).

KL Spike

A sudden large divergence from the reference policy, often after a few thousand steps. The policy jumps to a high-reward region but the reward model has poor coverage there, causing instability. Frequently correlated with learning rate spikes or gradient explosions.

Mitigations: gradient clipping (1.0), warm restart of β, smaller learning rate (≤1e-6 for 7B), monitor token-level KL per step.

Goodhart's Law in RL

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The reward model is a proxy for human preferences; optimising it directly will eventually over-fit to the proxy's imperfections. The Overoptimisation paper (Gao et al., 2023) showed that beyond ~5–10 × initial reward gain, gold-label human quality typically starts declining even as RM score keeps rising.

Compute Reality — Three-Model VRAM, Distributed

Full RLHF with PPO requires four model copies loaded simultaneously: actor (training), critic (training), reference policy (frozen SFT), and reward model (frozen). In practice the actor and critic often share the backbone with separate output heads, reducing this to three distinct weight sets.

Actor (πθ)bf16 + Adam fp32Yes~98 GB (full); ~10 GB (QLoRA)

Distributed Strategies

For 70B+ models, DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 is standard: shard actor and critic states across all GPUs; keep ref policy and RM on separate GPU sets or offload to CPU RAM during the rollout phase. The rollout phase (inference) and the update phase (training) have different memory profiles — some pipelines alternate device allocation between phases.

TRL DeepSpeed config

TRL's PPOTrainer accepts a deepspeed config dict. Set stage: 3 for actor/critic sharding. Ref policy and RM can be loaded in stage: 0 (replicated) if they fit, or stage: 3 if not. Set offload_optimizer_device: cpu to move Adam state to RAM, freeing ~48 GB VRAM for a 7B actor at the cost of ~1.5× slower optimiser steps.

TRL Implementation Walkthrough

TRL (Transformer Reinforcement Learning, HuggingFace) provides PPOTrainer that handles rollout collection, reward scoring, and PPO updates. The minimal working loop is straightforward:

trl ≥0.9 PPOTrainer minimal loop
from trl import PPOTrainer, PPOConfig, AutoModelForCausalLMWithValueHead
from transformers import AutoTokenizer

config = PPOConfig(
    model_name="meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct",
    learning_rate=1e-6,
    batch_size=64,
    mini_batch_size=16,
    gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
    ppo_epochs=4,
    kl_penalty="kl",          # "kl", "abs", "mse", "full"
    init_kl_coef=0.05,
    target_kl=0.05,            # adaptive KL target
    adap_kl_ctrl=True,
    cliprange=0.2,
    vf_coef=0.1,
)

model = AutoModelForCausalLMWithValueHead.from_pretrained(config.model_name)
ref_model = AutoModelForCausalLMWithValueHead.from_pretrained(config.model_name)
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(config.model_name)

trainer = PPOTrainer(config, model, ref_model, tokenizer)

for batch in dataloader:
    queries      = batch["input_ids"]
    responses    = trainer.generate(queries, max_new_tokens=256)
    rewards      = [reward_model(q, r) for q, r in zip(queries, responses)]
    stats        = trainer.step(queries, responses, rewards)
    trainer.log_stats(stats, batch, rewards)
AutoModelForCausalLMWithValueHead

This TRL class wraps a standard causal LM and adds a linear value head on top of the last hidden state. The value head outputs V(x) — the critic's estimate of expected future reward from prompt x. During PPO the actor loss and critic loss are computed jointly; the original LM backbone weights are shared between both objectives.

When PPO Is Still the Right Tool

DPO (Deck 04) and its cousins have dramatically simplified preference-based training for many tasks. But PPO retains real advantages in specific settings.

PPO wins

  • Online exploration: the policy generates novel completions and gets feedback — off-policy DPO datasets may not cover the behaviours you care about
  • Non-differentiable rewards: code execution, formal verification, tool-use success, test-case pass rates — any reward that can't be computed from a static dataset requires the RL loop
  • Iterative reward improvement: the reward model can be retrained on new rollout data; PPO naturally cycles reward-data-policy
  • Long-horizon tasks: credit assignment across multi-step traces (chain-of-thought, agentic trajectories) is more natural in the RL framework

PPO loses

  • You already have preference data: DPO is cheaper, no RM needed, no rollouts
  • Single-turn short responses: most response quality can be captured from existing comparisons; rollout exploration adds cost with little benefit
  • Small teams / limited compute: PPO requires 3–4 model copies; DPO needs only 2
  • Instability tolerance is low: PPO has more failure modes and requires more careful tuning; DPO is simpler to stabilise
The DeepSeek-R1 data point

DeepSeek-R1 (2025) used a variant of online RL (GRPO — covered in Deck 04) against a verifiable reward (math/code correctness) without a learned reward model at all. This is PPO's best use case: when you have a ground-truth reward signal, the reward model is the weakest link in the chain and replacing it with a verifier unlocks dramatic gains.

09

What to Take Away

  • SFT imitates; RLHF optimises. RLHF provides a gradient signal from pairwise human comparisons, not from gold labels — this unlocks behaviours that imitation data can't encode.
  • The reward model is trained with Bradley-Terry loss on pairwise preference data. It outputs a scalar; its accuracy caps the quality of everything downstream.
  • PPO runs four roles simultaneously: actor (generates), critic (estimates value), reference policy (anchors KL), reward model (scores). VRAM is the hard constraint.
  • The KL penalty is mandatory. Without it the actor exploits reward model gaps within thousands of steps. Set β adaptively with a target KL of ~0.05 nats/token.
  • Monitor KL, reward mean, and response entropy every step. KL spikes and mode collapse are detectable early; catching them before they diverge saves days of training.
  • Goodhart's Law always applies. Reward model score and actual human quality diverge beyond ~5–10 × initial improvement. Use held-out human evals as a ground truth.
  • PPO is still the right tool when rewards are verifiable and non-differentiable — code execution, math, formal verification. DPO wins for static preference datasets.
